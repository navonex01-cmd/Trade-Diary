How to Build a Trading Journal That Makes Your Trade Reviews More Useful

After a trading session, you can usually find your profit or loss within seconds. Understanding the decisions behind that result takes a more complete record.
You may have followed your plan and lost money. You may have entered impulsively and finished with a profit. Both trades contain information worth reviewing, but the closing balance alone cannot explain how well you executed your approach.
A trading journal brings those details together. It records what you traded, why you entered, how you managed the position and what happened when you exited.
Whether you trade intraday or hold positions across several sessions, a consistent journal gives you a clearer basis for reviewing your own decisions.
What Should a Trading Journal Include?
A useful trading journal combines transaction details with the context behind them.
Start with the information needed to reconstruct the position:
* Instrument and market.
* Entry and exit dates and times.
* Trade direction and position size.
* Entry and exit prices.
* Fees and other applicable costs.
* Realised profit or loss.
* Strategy or setup name.
Then add the information that your transaction history may not capture: your reasoning, planned exit conditions, emotional state and any changes you made while managing the trade.
The goal is a record you can understand later without relying on memory.
Record the Plan Before the Outcome Is Known
One of the most useful journal habits is writing down your reasoning before the result can influence your explanation.
Describe the conditions that made the trade qualify under your approach. Record what would invalidate the idea and how you intended to manage the position.
Keep the entry specific enough to review.
“Strong setup” gives you little to examine later. “The entry conditions in my plan were present, and I intended to exit if the stated invalidation occurred” creates a clearer reference.
After the trade closes, compare your actions with that original plan. Explain any early exit, position adjustment or change in your reasoning.
This preserves the difference between what you intended and what you eventually did.
Separate Execution Quality From Financial Outcome
A single trade can produce a favourable result despite poor execution. Another can follow a reasonable process and still lose money.
Record both dimensions.
First, document the financial outcome accurately. Then assess whether the entry, position management and exit followed the rules you had established.
This prevents a profitable result from automatically excusing an impulsive decision. It also prevents every losing trade from being labelled a mistake.
Over time, this distinction gives your trading review more substance than a simple list of wins and losses.
Use Screenshots to Preserve What You Saw
Screenshots can make a trade easier to revisit, particularly when the decision depended on visual information.
Capture the relevant chart around the entry and exit where practical. Include the instrument, timeframe and timestamp, and annotate the information that influenced you.
Preserve the original context. A completed chart can make a price move look more predictable than it appeared in the moment.
When reviewing the trade, compare the image with your written explanation. Check whether the conditions you described were actually present and whether your actions matched your reasoning.
Compare Trades Consistently
Trade performance analysis becomes more useful when similar records are grouped together.
You might review positions by strategy, instrument, intended holding period or another category relevant to your approach. Apply those categories consistently so that the meaning of each group remains stable.
Look beyond total P&L. Consider the number of trades, average winning and losing results, position sizes, costs and adherence to your plan.
A small group of successful trades is a starting point for investigation. It is not enough on its own to establish a dependable pattern.
Create a Weekly Trading Review
A repeatable review can follow four practical steps:
1. Check accuracy. Resolve missing trades, incorrect quantities and incomplete costs.
2. Review comparable groups. Examine results within your chosen categories.
3. Inspect individual decisions. Read the original plan alongside the execution notes.
4. Choose one process action. Identify a specific behaviour to monitor next week.
For example, you might decide to document the reason for every unplanned position adjustment. At the next review, check whether you maintained that record.
Keep the process manageable enough to repeat.
